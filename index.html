    Protein is the exception

    If you lose weight without enough protein, you lose muscle along with it. Over fifty that happens faster than before, and it's exactly what your strength training is trying to protect.

    For you: 150 grams per day. Calculate it on your target weight, not your current weight — fat tissue needs no protein. That comes to about 1.7 grams per kilo: deliberately a bit above the usual 1.2–1.6, because you're in a deficit, training hard and over fifty.

    In practice you get there without powder: three eggs is 18 grams, 200 g skyr or quark is 22, 150 g chicken or fish is 35, a tin of tuna is 25. That already makes a hundred — you get the last fifty with one or two extra portions, for example a shake.

    Your check: if your lean mass stays around 73 kg while your weight drops, you're on track. If it drops too, protein is the first thing to increase.

    Why you don't need to count
    • Fat and protein keep you full longer. Eggs with avocado keep you full for four to five hours; toast lasts an hour and a half. Less hunger automatically means eating less, without willpower.
    • Fewer blood sugar spikes, fewer cravings. The urge to snack at four o'clock is almost always a dip after a carb-heavy lunch.
    • Your body regulates better. With enough fat and protein, your satiety signals work as they should.

    What you do watch: nuts. They're calorie-dense and easy to keep eating — a handful is fine, half a bag is not. Fat from avocado or olive oil limits itself, nuts from a bag don't. Otherwise, no weighing grams.

    What you measure
    How to measure your waist correctly

    On bare skin, standing and relaxed. The tape goes halfway between your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone — for most people roughly at navel height. Horizontal all around, not at an angle.

    Measure at the end of a normal exhale, without pulling in your stomach and without tightening the tape. It should touch the skin but not press in. Always do it at the same time of day, preferably in the morning before eating — then your measurements are comparable.

    Strength
    Intensity

    The load is the stimulus, not the number of sets!

    • Is it heavy enough? Stop every working set — not just the last one — when you could still have done about two technically good reps. Choose the weight on your first set and keep it for all sets; with fatigue the number of reps usually drops 1–2 per set (e.g. 11, 10, 9). That's fine. If you drop below the bottom of the range, it was too heavy or you rested too briefly. If it feels like you could have done five more, it's too light. On isolation exercises your last set may go to 0–1 in reserve; on the heavy exercises you stay at 2.
    • Stay within the range of your plan. Each exercise has its own range: 6–10 for the heaviest, 8–12 or 10–15 for the rest. Lighter weight with more reps builds as much muscle as heavier with fewer, as long as you get close to failure. A weight you can move with good form is therefore no compromise, provided you stop with about two reps in reserve.
    • When to increase? First add reps at the same weight. Once you reach the top of the range on all sets (e.g. 12 in 8–12), the weight goes up and you start at the bottom again (around 8). You never go above it — you climb within the range and reset with more weight. Only when that path stalls do you add a fourth set.
    • Rest fully. Two to three minutes on the heavy exercises, ninety seconds on isolation. Resting too briefly makes your next set lighter than intended.
    Tempo

    Slowly back to the starting point.

    • The rule: the phase where you resist the weight should be slower. The other phase can be brisk. On a curl that means curling up briskly, lowering to straight slowly. On a squat that means lowering slowly, standing back up briskly. About one to two counts for the brisk phase, two to three counts for the slow one.
    • It's also the safe way to do it. Tendon injuries happen with a sudden jolt on a loaded joint, not with a gradually built-up load. As long as you control the lowering phase, that moment doesn't exist. Swinging to get the weight up, or dropping it at the end of a set, are the two moments where you lose control.
    • Never swing. If you use momentum to get the weight up, you lose control exactly at the heaviest point — and that's where injuries happen. If it doesn't work without swinging, the weight is too heavy.
    • Don't overdo it. Five counts or more costs you weight without giving more results, and causes a lot more muscle soreness.
    Cardio
    Form and zone

    2× per week, 60 minutes, separate from your strength training. Any form — only your heart rate counts. Train in HR zone 2 as shown by the Apple Watch. Doesn't have to be in the gym: outdoor walk-jogging or cycling works just as well. Exception: during your VO2max blocks your heart rate can deliberately peak higher, up to zone 4–5.

    Your cardio plan: outdoor walking with running blocks

    An hour of brisk outdoor walking is your zone 2 base: fat-burning, joint-friendly, with daylight and fresh air on top. Throw in a tougher block now and then — up a hill, or a few minutes faster.

    Those blocks lift your heart rate for a while (more fitness and VO2max stimulus than flat walking). If jogging suits your knee, it also gives the most bone stimulus; with your meniscus, uphill walking is the knee-friendly alternative that still loads you.

    If you really want to raise your VO2max

    Easy walking builds your base, but only lifts your VO2max a little. For real gains, make the blocks shorter and harder: 4× 4 minutes almost all-out (zone 4–5, you can no longer talk) with 3 minutes of easy walking in between each. Knee-friendly as a steep hill or an inclined treadmill, so without jogging.

    One such session per week is enough. VO2max is one of the strongest predictors of healthy years of life — at your level (±33) there's a lot to gain with little effort. Build up gradually: start with loose running blocks, make them tougher as it feels easier.

    Keep at least one cardio day with impact

    Walk-jogging or running gives a bone stimulus through the light impact — valuable at your age. Rowing, cross-training and cycling are joint-friendly but without impact.

    The talk test beats the table

    If you're in a higher HR zone while breathing calmly, that's no problem — your breathing is the measure.

    Where you want to be: short sentences work, a conversation doesn't. That feeling is more reliable than any number.

    Even simpler: if you can keep breathing through your nose the whole time, you're good. If you have to switch to your mouth, you're going too hard.
